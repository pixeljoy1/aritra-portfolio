## At a glance
- **Question:** how did two games change what PCs could draw, and what PCs people bought?
- **What I did:** researched Doom (1993) and Quake (1996), compared them feature by feature, and presented the findings in a deck styled as an arcade title screen: "Player 1, Player 2, Highscore 2500".
- **What I found:** Doom made 3D feel possible with clever software. Quake made real 3D the standard and pushed the industry towards graphics cards.

## Doom (1993): a new era of PC graphics
Doom was a dark, maze-like arena with monsters, weapons and mood lighting. It looked 3D but was really **2.5D**: walls and floors were flat shapes made to look three-dimensional by the way the player moved. That is why it was fast and ran on modest hardware. It stood out because it was smooth, immersive and even let people play together. It became so popular that people upgraded their PCs just to play it, which showed that graphics could drive hardware sales.

## Quake (1996): true 3D arrives
Quake used a fully 3D engine: characters, weapons and levels were built from **polygons**. It added lightmaps for more realistic shadows and atmosphere, smoother textures, and support for **OpenGL** hardware acceleration so graphics cards could share the work with the CPU. You could look and move in every direction. It pushed PCs so hard that it helped force the rise of 3D graphics cards such as the 3dfx Voodoo.

## Side by side
| | Doom | Quake |
|---|---|---|
| Graphics | 2.5D tricks | Fully 3D models and environments |
| Enemies | Flat 2D sprites | 3D polygonal models |
| Lighting | Simple shading | Pre-calculated lightmaps |
| Hardware | Ran on the CPU alone | Needed new 3D graphics cards |
| Legacy | Popularised the first-person shooter | Set the standard for 3D games and GPUs |

## Why it matters
Doom showed that clever software could create the illusion of 3D and fully immerse a player. Quake confirmed that the future was true 3D and hardware acceleration. Together they shaped later engines like Unreal and Unity and set the expectation of what "good graphics" means. Today's PC and console games, and immersive VR and AR, trace their roots to these two games.
