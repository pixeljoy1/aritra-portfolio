## At a glance
- **Context:** a public-facing portal run by a national government agency, used by very different people, from first-time users to retirees.
- **What I did:** I joined the research team as a special invitee for about a month in January 2025, in the run-up to the final report. I worked as a **note-taker** and **observer**, ran much of the **research operations** around the sessions (recording, processing, logging and tabulating), and also took part as a **test participant**, so I saw the study from both sides of the table.
- **What came out:** a report of findings and recommendations that the team delivered. The recommendations here are the kind it contained; the numbers and screens are not mine to share.

## The question
Can a person reach their goal on this portal with little help, and where do they get stuck? The study set out to evaluate usability, find what to improve, and give recommendations the owner could act on, using both what people said and what they did.

## Method
It was a **mixed-methods, moderated usability study**, run both in person and remotely.
- **Who:** a deliberately wide range of people, varied in age, occupation and digital confidence: blue-collar workers, working professionals, business owners, students and retirees. The hardest cases for a design are often the quietest voices in a sample, so they were included on purpose.
- **How a session ran:** a short questionnaire about familiarity, then realistic scenario tasks, then an interview and a closing System Usability Scale. Each session took under an hour, on laptops, phones and tablets, with audio, video and written observations kept.
- **The six scenarios:** understand what the portal offers from the home page; register an account; log in with a one-time code; search with some details; recover from errors such as a blank or partial search; reset a forgotten password.
- **After every task:** one question, "how easy was that?", on a seven-point scale, with probing questions to find the reason behind the rating.

## My part
I covered the whole research-operations side, so the moderators could stay focused on the person in front of them.
- **Note-taker.** Capturing what people did, in order, and what they said, without interpreting it too early. Good notes are what the analysis stands on.
- **Observer.** Watching hesitation, backtracking and workarounds: the moments where a person does something different from what the screen expected.
- **Recording.** Making sure sessions, in person and over video, were captured and archived with audio, video and notes together.
- **Logging.** Keeping one session tracker: who, when, which profile, in person or remote, who moderated, who took notes, what was done and what was said in the daily debrief.
- **Processing.** Cleaning the raw notes after each session and tagging each issue to the task where it happened.
- **Tabulating.** Entering the ease ratings and the ten SUS answers per participant, and keeping the scoring workbook that turned them into scores.
- **Participant.** Doing the tasks myself showed me what it feels like to be watched, and why moderators must stay neutral.

### How the SUS sheet works
The System Usability Scale has ten statements rated from one to five. Odd-numbered statements are worded positively, even-numbered ones negatively, so each answer is adjusted before adding up: odd answers lose one point, even answers are taken away from five. The adjusted answers are added and multiplied by 2.5 to give a score out of 100. The workbook did this with formulas, so scores, grade bands and group averages updated as each participant was entered.

## Where I made a difference
- **One source of truth.** A single tracker meant anyone on the team could see who had been tested, how, by whom, and what was still to do, without asking.
- **Notes the analysis could trust.** Same-day processing, with each issue tagged to a task, kept the findings tied to what people actually did.
- **Scores that updated themselves.** Formulas for the adjusted answers, grade bands and averages removed manual arithmetic and made errors easy to spot.
- **Patterns visible early.** Because every session was logged and tabulated straight away, the daily debrief could point at trends while there was still time to probe them in the next sessions.

## What the study showed
**What worked.** A clean, uncluttered layout. One-time codes that arrived quickly. A purpose that was clear on first contact. People felt it was easy after a try or two.

**Where people struggled**
- **First contact:** little guidance for first-timers, and a key action that was easy to miss.
- **Forms:** rules for names and passwords that did not match how people really write them, a hidden way to check a password, and a CAPTCHA that was hard to read.
- **Search and results:** unclear inputs, and results that made some people worry they were seeing other people's information.
- **Errors and confirmations:** messages that vanished before they could be read, and confirmations that passed too quickly.
- **Readability:** small text and icons, hardest for older users. For a financial task, some people also preferred a bigger screen.

## What the recommendations looked like
Add a short explainer and a visible help route. Make the main action unmistakable. Let forms accept real names and real passwords, with a visible show-password control. Keep errors and confirmations on screen until the person acts. Use a friendlier CAPTCHA. Separate a person's own results from general information. Raise text size and contrast. Offer an easy FAQ.

## What I took away
- Test with the people a design is most likely to fail, not just the easiest to recruit.
- Separate observation from interpretation: write down what happened first, then ask why.
- Good research operations are invisible when they work: the tracker, the recordings and the sheets are what let a team move fast without losing rigour.
- Every recommendation should trace to a task and a goal the person was trying to reach.
- A report people act on is short, specific and honest about what it cannot say.
